# ADR-003 — In-Memory Transfer Engine

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-10-18

## Context
We are benchmarking in-memory transfer engines.
The setup includes several parameters
Number of threads : 1000
Number of transfers per thread : 1000
Work (estimated time in critical section, simulated with parkNanos) : 20 µs (actually takes 28 on this Mac)
Number of accounts : 10 or 1000
Core invariants to be preserved are the following:
- The sum of the balances is always constant
- There is no negative balance
We need to find options to preserve the core invariants at all time including for readers, then maximizes the throughput.


## Options
1. Naive -> Rejected: Core invariant broken 15 times out of 15
2. Unordered Lock -> Rejected: Deadlock and freeze the whole system
3. Global Lock -> Rejected: preserved the core invariants and is actually faster than OrderedLock at work=0 (≈ 3.7 M vs ≈ 2.4 M). 
But every single thread has to acquire the global lock so the maximum throughput is `1/time of critical section`. At 29 microseconds, the ceiling is ≈ 34,500/s no matter the number of accounts. Measured: 34,659 / 34,489 / 34,402 per second at 10 / 1,000 / 10,000 accounts
By contrast, the ceiling of OrderedLockEngine grows with the number of accounts. Measured: 28,044 / 43,833 / 73,633 (close to the lock-free benchmark's ceiling) per second at 10 / 1,000 / 100,000 accounts.
Real transfers never have zero work under the lock, and real banks have millions of accounts. The global lock stays stuck at ≈ 34,500/s whereas OrderedLockEngine scales.
4. Ordered Lock -> Accepted: preserved the core invariant all times and transfers are atomic. 
5. Atomic -> Rejected: Does not preserve the core invariants during transfer but only at quiescence. Lock-based engines hold locks, Atomic holds nothing
6. Queue -> Rejected: Does not preserve the core invariants during transfer but only at quiescence; at 10 accounts, up to 99% of money in flight and 77% of transfer rejected

## Decision
- Transfers use OrderedLock engine which preserves the invariant
- The locks are always acquired in the same order for writes or reads
- The business rule is checked and the transfer is made within the try block, followed by the release of the locks in the finally block
- The total is always consistent since the read acquires all the locks
- The engine relies on Reentrant locks so virtual threads are not pinned


## Consequences and trade offs
- Core invariants and business rules are preserved.
- No deadlocks between transfers
- No lost updates
- Total is always consistent
- Throughput: at work 20, ordered is ~1.8× slower than atomic (43,833/s vs 78,915/s), because both locks are held during the work.
- A consistent read blocks every transfer while it holds all the locks, and lockAll() grows with the number of accounts: fine for 1,000, impossible for 65 M. At scale, a consistent read needs a snapshot (MVCC) or a ledger.
-  The atomic engine must validate both accounts before the first CAS; it didn’t, and an unknown `to` destroyed money (fixed in 3a8ec53)
- None of these engines are persistent. A crash loses everything
- Locks can never be acquired in another way anywhere in the code base
- A hot account could degrade performance since many threads will be waiting to acquire its lock


## When to choose another option
- More work must stay under the lock, or lock waits grow → atomic engine, or move the work out of the lock
- One hot account dominates → per-account queue or append-only ledger
- Consistent reads are frequent → snapshot or ledger, not lockAll().
- More than one JVM, or durability required → the database (ADR-001), partitioned by account_id
